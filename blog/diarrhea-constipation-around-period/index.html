Diarrhea or constipation around your period: what can help?

Published September 6, 2026·by Chanwook Kim, MiniCycle developer·3 min read

At a glance

Diarrhea or constipation can occur around your period, and cycle-related changes may play a part. But the timing alone does not tell you the cause. Mild symptoms may respond to simple care; blood in the stool, severe pain, or signs of dehydration need prompt medical attention.

Why can bowel symptoms happen around a period?

The Office on Women’s Health PMS guide lists constipation and diarrhea among possible premenstrual symptoms. Hormone changes may contribute to PMS, but its exact cause is not fully understood. Not everyone experiences the same bowel changes each month.

ACOG’s period pain guide explains that prostaglandins cause uterine contractions and that painful periods can come with diarrhea or nausea. This helps explain why cramps and digestive discomfort can occur together; it does not prove that every episode of diarrhea is caused by your period.

If you want to understand the wider pattern of symptoms before bleeding starts, our PMS guide explains how timing and effects on daily life are considered together.

If stools are loose, focus on fluids first

NIDDK’s diarrhea treatment guide recommends replacing lost fluids and electrolytes. Drink fluids; an oral rehydration solution may help replace losses. If you have kidney disease, diabetes, or another condition affecting fluid needs, ask a clinician about the appropriate drink and amount.

When your appetite returns, you can usually return to your normal diet. If you have fever or bloody stools, seek medical advice rather than trying to manage the episode with an over-the-counter antidiarrheal.

If you are constipated, keep the approach gentle

NIDDK’s constipation guidance recommends fiber-containing foods, enough liquids, regular physical activity, and going when you feel the urge. Give yourself time to use the bathroom. These are general constipation measures, not a treatment for a proven hormone problem.

If you suspect a medicine or supplement is contributing, speak with a clinician before changing it. Ask for advice if self-care is not helping rather than repeatedly trying different laxatives on your own.

When should you seek medical help?

NIDDK’s diarrhea warning signs include severe abdominal or rectal pain, bloody or black tarry stools, frequent vomiting, and dehydration. Markedly reduced urination, a dry mouth, or dizziness can be signs of dehydration. Seek prompt medical care for these symptoms.

For adults, contact a clinician promptly if diarrhea lasts more than two days, comes with high fever, or involves six or more loose stools a day. Do not wait for a duration threshold when symptoms are severe. An infection or a medicine side effect can coincide with a period.

Pain when passing stool deserves attention too

Arrange a medical appointment if bowel pain repeatedly returns with periods, symptoms are worsening, or they interfere with ordinary activities. The NHS endometriosis guide lists pain when passing stool among possible symptoms. That symptom alone does not establish an endometriosis diagnosis.

Describe both bowel symptoms and pelvic pain to the clinician, including whether they also occur outside your period. Our period pain guide explains why changes in pain are worth discussing.

What is useful to note before an appointment?

A short description is enough: when the bowel change began, whether stools were loose or hard, how often you went, where it hurt, and whether it happened before or after bleeding started. Mention recent medicines or food changes and the effect on your day. Do not delay care to complete a diary.

Fictional example for explaining symptoms: “Loose stools began the day before bleeding. On day one I went three times and missed my commute because of cramps. By day three my usual bowel pattern had returned.” This is a communication example, not a definition of what is normal.

Our guide to preparing for a period-related medical appointment can help organize the conversation. The important point is whether the change is brief and manageable or persistent, painful, or disrupting daily life, not simply whether it arrived with your period.
