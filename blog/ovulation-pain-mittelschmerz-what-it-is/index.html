Ovulation pain (mittelschmerz): what that mid-cycle twinge is

Published July 3, 2026·by Chanwook Kim, MiniCycle developer·Updated September 2, 2026·3 min read

At a glance

Some people feel brief, one-sided lower-abdominal pain around ovulation. This is called ovulation pain or mittelschmerz, but timing and sensation alone cannot confirm that ovulation caused a particular pain. Severe, persistent, recurrent, or pregnancy-associated pelvic pain needs medical assessment because other conditions can look similar.

The one-sided ache that can appear mid-cycle

Mittelschmerz means ‘middle pain’ and describes pelvic or lower-abdominal pain that can occur around ovulation. It is often felt on one side, but the timing and location alone cannot confirm that ovulation is the cause.

The side may vary between cycles, and the ovaries do not necessarily alternate in a predictable pattern.

What it can feel like, and how long it may last

The pain may feel dull, cramp-like, or briefly sharp. Some people also notice cervical fluid changes or light spotting, but those signs have other possible explanations.

NHS and Mayo Clinic describe episodes ranging from minutes to a day or two. Pain that is severe, lasts longer, worsens, or occurs with other concerning symptoms should be medically assessed.

Why releasing an egg may hurt

An egg develops inside a fluid-filled follicle. Mayo Clinic lists two possible explanations for pain: stretching of the ovary's surface as the follicle grows, and irritation from a small amount of blood or fluid released when it ruptures.

The exact mechanism is not settled. Pain timing cannot identify the exact moment of ovulation or prove that ovulation occurred.

It doesn't mean you're more fertile, and it isn't birth control

Two myths ride along with ovulation pain. The first is that feeling it means you are especially fertile. Cleveland Clinic is blunt about this: ovulation pain does not make you more fertile and does not change your odds of conceiving that cycle. It is a sensation, not an advantage.

The second is that you can use the twinge to avoid pregnancy by steering clear of that one day. You cannot rely on it that way. The pain is approximate, the fertile window spans several days, and sperm can outlive the ache. If you are trying to conceive, a twinge is a useful nudge that ovulation is near; if you are trying not to, it is not a method.

To compare pain with other observations, read Ovulation signs and basal body temperature: the basics.

When pelvic pain needs assessment

Mild, typical ovulation pain often resolves on its own. Before taking an over-the-counter medicine, consider pregnancy possibility, medical conditions, other medicines, and the product instructions; ask a pharmacist or clinician if you are unsure.

Seek prompt medical assessment for sudden or severe one-sided pain, pain with possible pregnancy, fainting, fever, vomiting, painful urination, or heavy bleeding. Persistent or frequently recurring pain also deserves evaluation because ovarian cysts, ovarian torsion, appendicitis, pelvic inflammatory disease, and ectopic pregnancy can cause similar symptoms.

Record the timing without self-diagnosing

Note the date, side, duration, severity, and accompanying symptoms over several cycles. A recurring mid-cycle pattern may be useful context, but it still does not rule out another cause.
