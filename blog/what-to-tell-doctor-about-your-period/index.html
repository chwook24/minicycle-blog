What to tell your doctor about your period: a tracking guide

Published June 15, 2026·by Chanwook Kim, MiniCycle developer·Updated November 16, 2026·4 min read

At a glance

Bring actual period start and end dates, symptom notes, and the main question you want to ask. Use the history you already have; you do not need a complete diary before seeking care. Mark uncertain dates as uncertain and keep predictions separate from recorded bleeding. Do not postpone an appointment just to collect more weeks of data.

Start with the history you have

Gather the dates and notes you can confirm now, whether they are on paper or in a phone. If a date is missing, say so rather than inventing one to make the history look complete.

You can keep recording while waiting for the appointment. Do not delay booking or urgent care in order to build several weeks of entries. A short, honest history is still something you can bring to the conversation.

The dates that carry the most weight

Write down each period's first day and its last day of bleeding. Mayo Clinic counts cycle length from one period's first day to the next period's first day; bleeding duration is a separate number.

Fictional example: bleeding starts September 4 and ends September 9, and the next period starts October 5. The bleeding lasts 6 days when both the first and last day are counted; the start-to-start interval is 31 days. Bring the original dates as well as any calculation. These example numbers are not targets to match.

What to note beyond the dates

When you are worried about your periods, Mayo Clinic lists a few things worth recording alongside the dates: how long the period lasts, the flow and how often you change a pad or tampon and whether you have passed clots, any bleeding between periods, pain and whether it is worse than usual, and any change in mood or anything new that happened around the time things shifted. None of it needs to be more than a few words.

Life outside the cycle earns a line too, because it explains the odd month later. A bad illness, a stretch of short sleep, a long flight across time zones, a new medication. A note from the month it happened lets you and the clinician connect the two instead of guessing.

The changes worth flagging

Mayo Clinic recommends medical review for periods less than 24 or more than 38 days apart, bleeding beyond 8 days, a new irregular pattern, bleeding between periods, severe pain, or periods stopping for more than 90 days when not pregnant. Age, contraception, and life stage matter; discuss a worrying change even if a number falls within a reference range.

The Office on Women's Health frames the why in one line: irregular, painful, or heavy periods can be signs of a health problem, and a doctor can work with you on them. Walking in able to point to the month a 90-day gap started beats a sense that it has been a while.

For very heavy flow and urgent accompanying symptoms, read heavy-period signs and when to seek help.

Bringing it to the appointment

Bring the record in a form you can open or read during the visit, such as your phone or a paper copy. Put your main question alongside it: for example, what changed, when it changed, and how it affects your day.

MiniCycle shows recorded start dates, recent cycle and period-length summaries, and daily notes. Records are stored on the device by default; if iCloud Sync is enabled, a copy is also stored in your iCloud through Apple CloudKit.

What the record can't settle

Bring saved dates as your history, and label predicted dates as estimates. MiniCycle's calendar forecasts are not a medical diagnosis or contraception. The guide to period-tracker prediction limits explains why a forecast should stay separate from a record of what actually happened.

So the app shows the pattern clearly; the clinician is the one who reads meaning into it. Bring the record as the starting point for that conversation, not as a stand-in for it. The dates make the appointment faster and more concrete, and that is the whole job they have.
